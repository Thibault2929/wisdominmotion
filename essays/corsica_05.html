Day four

The first light of day entering the building woke me up. Even though I pulled my shirt over my eyes to remain in complete darkness, I could feel that it was slowly time to leave. After stretching my muscles and simply enjoying the fact that I had spent the night in an abandoned hotel, I quietly stood up, careful not to wake Émilie, and began packing my things. "Are you going to leave?" she asked, opening her eyes while remaining nestled inside her sleeping bag. "Yes." "I wish you good luck. Have fun." "You too. Enjoy your day at the waterfalls." With that, she closed her eyes once more and settled back into her sleeping bag, resting her head on the bundle of clothes that had served as a pillow. I put on the shirt I had hung on a rope the previous evening. The other guest of the abandoned Grand Hôtel was also packing his belongings, preparing to leave. As he stood up, he finally noticed me. We exchanged a brief smile before quickly leaving the building, each following his own path. Outside, the area was already beginning to buzz with hikers from the nearby campsites, all eager to press on.

The trail soon narrowed beneath the pines before climbing steeply, and the gentle forest floor gradually gave way to bare granite rock. The stream running alongside the path quickly transformed into a lively mountain torrent, tumbling over countless waterfalls and carving deep, crystal-clear pools into the massive granite—the famous Cascade des Anglais. I could easily picture Émilie spending the day there, reading a book, occasionally taking a refreshing bath before warming herself once again on the sun-baked stone. I almost envied her. But as my body and mind enjoyed the challenging climb, I was reminded that I had come here for hiking. Being curious once more about what lay ahead, the thought of taking a bath myself soon vanished. I did, however, allow my thoughts to wander while filling up my flasks. I pictured myself slipping into one of the cold pools while my arms rested on the warm granite. How the day could have been if I had decided to rest instead of climbing up. That much, at least, I permitted myself.

I soon left the torrent behind, and silence took over as I headed towards the top. There, the mountain pass was already crowded with other hikers, and the air was filled with their chatter. I decided to also have a short halt, eating some bread and cheese while drinking water from the flasks I had just filled. It was especially warm today and, with temperatures not having reached their peak yet, I was aware that I would have to be prudent with my water reserves. But I knew that the Refuge de l'Onda was down in the valley below, and that I'd be able to refill my flasks there. While watching hikers coming and going, I was quite annoyed by a group of young people, fully equipped with trail running gear, one of them putting pressure on another who was still making his way to the top. "Allez, allez, a bit faster now. We won't make it in time if you don't pick up the pace." This remark was followed by him analysing his sports watch and comparing his pace to that of his friend. "Well, we'll have a short break of 10 minutes maximum, and then we need to go. We need to leave soon with the current pace we have" No one in the group replied to that. At that moment, I asked him where in France they were from, to which he replied, "Paris." I was not surprised. I could not imagine someone from a rural area putting such pressure on others while being in nature. I tried to exchange a few words, but, realising that the young man was either not the best of listeners or simply not interested in talking to me, I soon gave up, packed my things, and pressed on. The way down was surprisingly easy, as that side of the mountain was covered with thick soil and grass. I ran down with much joy, and soon enough I found myself at the very bottom of the valley. Yet I was confused. I couldn't spot the refuge, even though it should have been very close. I walked farther down the valley and still couldn't find it. Not only that, I soon lost sight of the GR 20 and didn't manage to find it again. I did, however, spot two hikers coming from a rocky trail that led up the mountain on the opposite side from the one I had just descended. With temperatures rising and feeling neither hungry nor tired, I decided to walk in their direction. As I crossed paths with them, I asked whether this was the continuation of the GR 20. "Yes, it's the trail towards Refuge de Petra Piana," they told me. I thanked them and pressed on. It was hard to walk fast, as the ground was slippery due to the blanket of small rocks covering the steep mountain flank. It was now truly hot and, with limited water supplies while sweating heavily, I soon realised that I had to slow down if I wanted to prevent dehydration. As I reached the mountain ridge, I granted myself a brief halt, and while I drank carefully from my flask, I could spot the Refuge de l'Onda in the distance. I understood that I had missed it because it had been hidden by a slope separating it from the path I had taken on my way down. Being focused on running, I had probably missed it, as I was mostly looking at the ground. I sighed, knowing now that I would not be able to taste the lasagne that hikers had recommended to me. What I didn't know was that this, perhaps, was the work of my guardian angel. It was only later that I read about many hikers falling ill around the time I passed this part of the trail, for they had stopped at the refuge and drunk its water, which was contaminated at the time. I was therefore spared diarrhoea, and worse, by sheer luck. Oh Fortuna, I thank you! As I took a quick look at the map, I realised that I was on yet another variant, one that took me over the Pointe de la Pinzi Corbini instead of heading west, down the valley, before eventually turning east again towards Petra Piana. I grinned as I saw the two water sources marked on the map along the normal route. None of that for me. I would have to press on until I reached the refuge for water. And with that, I drank the last sip that remained in my flask. I slowed my pace, tried not to sweat, and enjoyed the rocky terrain, which required all four limbs in certain sections. I crossed only five more hikers. One of them, a middle-aged woman whose outfit reminded me of Indiana Jones, was accompanied by her dog, who seemed to enjoy the hike even though he was panting heavily due to the heat and the demanding terrain. I saw that she was carrying a rope around her shoulder, and I wondered whether she had used it already to help her companion across some of the more difficult sections. It didn't take much longer for me to spot the refuge which lay at the end of the mountains flank. And just like the others, it was surrounded by an armada of white tents. I was now very thirsty and looking forward to taking a refreshing sip. Leaving the rocky ground behind me and entering the refuge's grounds, I soon came upon a stream from which I filled my flasks and took several hearty gulps with my hands. I wetted my bandana and walked over to the main building where I hoped to find a spot in the shade. I ordered some pasta with tomato sauce, herbs, and an Orangina, which had started to become my regular drink at lunchtime. I took a seat at one of the tables while savouring the landscape. To the north lay Monte Rotondo, hidden from sight, but one could sense what lay behind the cliffs embracing the refuge. The west was also hidden, even though it was not very far to the mountain pass. Beyond it lay the Guagno valley, which I would come to see as I pursued my hike towards Punta Muzzella to the northwest. To the east lay the Gialgo Ravine, which the normal route of the GR 20 followed, and one could spot the Bergerie de Gialgo in the near distance. Its basin was covered with trees through which a river flowed. To the south lay the high point of Pinzi Corbini and its rocky, dry flanks, the alternative path from which I had just come. The refuge itself consisted of several buildings and even had a concrete platform on which stood four wooden huts that served as dry toilets for the guests; one had to operate a mechanical pedal so that one's belongings would eventually disappear on a rolling belt. Mules roamed freely, and it occasionally happened that they would poke their heads into the A-frame dormitory before someone eventually pushed them away. I was also lucky that some Corsicans happened to be at the refuge. They were doing a day hike and, having already consumed a few beers, one of them grabbed a guitar and started playing a melody to which he and his companions began singing in Corsican. "One more, but then we really do have to go back down," said the one playing the guitar as the others asked him to play yet another song. He ended up playing for another half an hour until, finally looking at the sky, he decided that it was now or never to head back down the valley. I appreciated their relaxed attitude, carrying little and clearly drunk, yet determined to head back, where I assumed someone would drive them home by car despite him not being entirely sober. As they left, I decided to lie down on the grass near the A-frame building and have a wee nap before resuming my journey, for the next section seemed challenging, and I wanted my mind to be fully rested so that it could focus entirely on what lay ahead.
